# Professor and team review — cryptocurrency economic classification

15 October 2026

## One-sentence objective

Build a reproducible way to describe what a cryptocurrency actually does economically, then test whether those functions help explain value and risk.

## What has been built

- A verified matrix covering 23 cryptocurrencies and ten economic functions: 230 evidence-backed decisions.
- Written rules for monetary use, gas, staking, burn, scarcity, collateral, governance, protocol capture, utility, and incentives.
- Effective dating so a mechanism is never applied to market data before it became active.
- Asset-level advisory profiles showing economic identity, possible value channels, cautions, and economic peers.
- Empirical tests for architecture, network usage, fee capture, supply events, staking readiness, valuation groups, and risk.
- A 91-command reproducible pipeline with 365 passing tests.

## The clearest findings

### 1. Architecture helps, but is incomplete

**What we did:** Compared all 253 unique pairs in the 23-asset universe across the ten functions.

**What we found:** Same-architecture pairs share 51.7% of functions on average, versus 33.1% for different architectures. The difference is statistically clear, but HBAR–TAO have identical economic profiles despite different architectures and BNB–TRX overlap by 88.9%.

**What it means:** Architecture is useful technical context. It cannot replace direct economic classification when selecting comparables.

### 2. Function composition is more informative than function count

**What we did:** Compared a raw count of functions with theory-defined economic groups.

**What we found:** Financial integration lowers leave-one-asset-out valuation error from 1.8109 to 1.7594, an improvement of 2.8%.

**What it means:** It matters which economic jobs a token performs, not simply how many. The improvement is modest and exploratory, not a price target.

### 3. Usage and holder capture are different

**What we did:** Tested whether fee activity has a stronger valuation association when a capture mechanism is active.

**What we found:** Under the broad capture definition, the interaction is 0.132 with exact p = 0.082. Under the strict rule requiring value to terminate at the token or holder, it falls to 0.061 with p = 0.478.

**What it means:** Governance over a treasury is not the same as receiving value. The definition determines the apparent result.

### 4. Supply events do not provide a simple trading rule

**What we did:** Tested 17 material unlock and burn events across ARB, BNB, and UNI.

**What we found:** Eight of 17 events move in the predicted direction; every asset-level average opposes H3. Exact asset-level p = 0.25.

**What it means:** An unlock or burn announcement is not enough to infer a short-window return direction.

### 5. Staking remains an open test

**What we did:** Built BNB consensus-stake history, market controls, a separate legacy stkAAVE case, and an exact ETH collector.

**What we found:** No H4 coefficient exists because historical ETH active effective balance is still unavailable.

**What it means:** The project explicitly refuses to substitute a weak proxy merely to produce a result.

## What is usable today

- A consistent vocabulary for explaining cryptoassets to clients.
- A due-diligence checklist that separates economic demand, supply effects, rights, and capture.
- Economic-peer selection based on function overlap.
- A way to challenge claims such as “governance means cash flow” or “a burn must increase price.”
- A transparent record of what is known, unsupported, or missing.

## What is not supported today

- Fair-value estimates or price targets.
- Automatic investment scores.
- Portfolio weights.
- Buy/sell signals.
- Causal statements that a function creates returns.

## Methodological strengths

- Rules are written before cases are coded.
- Missing evidence is not converted into a zero.
- Classifications are effective-dated.
- Small-sample inference uses exact permutations or exact sign assignments where feasible.
- Alternative definitions and null findings are retained.
- Every reported result is generated by code from archived inputs.

## Main limitations

- One researcher coded 220 of the 230 cells without an independent second coder.
- Only ten cells have an earlier blind review.
- H1 and H8 still need genuinely later validation periods.
- H3 has only three independent event assets.
- H4 is blocked by ETH archive data.
- The valuation associations are exploratory and non-causal.

## Questions for the professor or industry reviewers

1. Is the strict definition of holder capture appropriate: must value mechanically reach the token or holder?
2. Are the monetary-use requirements sufficiently material and observable?
3. Is the collateral threshold—1% of market capitalization or $100 million for 90 days—appropriate?
4. Should a fee be classified as gas only when consensus requires it, or when practical network inclusion normally requires it?
5. Are the eight economic groups a useful advisory abstraction?
6. Which part is most publishable: the classification instrument, the architecture result, or the capture-boundary result?

## Recommended meeting flow

1. **Problem — 1 minute:** architecture and marketing labels do not fully describe how a token accrues value.
2. **Method — 2 minutes:** ten functions, dated evidence, explicit rules, and effective dating.
3. **Results — 4 minutes:** architecture, function composition, capture sensitivity, and supply-event null.
4. **Advisory use — 1 minute:** due diligence and comparable selection, not recommendations.
5. **Limitations and decisions — 2 minutes:** external rule validation, the unsuccessful evidence-only fourth-asset screen, and ETH archive access.
